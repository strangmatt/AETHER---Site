SR-15099

“SR 2.0” — Biased Mu-Opioid Receptor Agonist

Harm Reduction Store

Dosage & Status

There is no established human dose for SR-15099. It has never been tested in a clinical trial, and no reliable dosing, duration, or safety data exist for people. Any numbers you see quoted online come from individual reports, not from research, and they cannot be verified. Its close analogue SR 17018 has a community-reported dosing history, but the two compounds are not interchangeable and doses should not be carried over from one to the other.

This page summarizes what the published animal research shows and what is not known, and it deliberately does not give a dose chart.

Overview

SR-15099, informally called "SR 2.0," is a synthetic opioid research compound developed in the laboratory of Laura Bohn at Scripps Research, first described in the scientific literature around 2017. It is a close structural analogue of SR-17018 and of the opioid brorphine: it is identical to SR-17018 except that the chlorine atom on the benzyl ring is replaced by a bromine atom. Like SR-17018, it is described as a non-competitive, G-protein-biased partial agonist of the mu-opioid receptor, meaning it engages the receptor's G-protein signaling pathway much more strongly than the beta-arrestin pathway associated with respiratory depression, constipation, and rapid tolerance with conventional opioids. It is unscheduled and unapproved, and remains a preclinical research chemical.

The two compounds are so similar that most of what is understood about SR-15099 is inferred from SR-17018 and from the small number of studies that examined both.

SR-15099 is much less studied than SR-17018. It is reasonable to expect similar behavior given the near-identical structure, but expectation is not evidence: a single atom change can meaningfully change potency, duration, metabolism, and safety.

Read the potency row carefully, because it is the row people get wrong. SR-15099 is roughly 60% less potent at producing G-protein signalling: it takes about 1.6 times as much drug to reach the same signal. Affinity for the receptor — how tightly it holds on, which is what keeps withdrawal symptoms down — is reported as essentially equal for the two compounds. So in principle SR-15099 can do the same job, but not at the same number of milligrams.

This is the most plausible explanation for why community reports on SR-15099 flatly contradict each other. People who dose it low, trying to avoid tolerance or blunting, report it barely touching withdrawal. People who dose higher report it working about as well as SR-17018, and a few report doing well on low doses. Several have reported needing roughly double their usual SR-17018 amount. A potency ratio from a cell assay is not a dose conversion, and this page still does not give a dose chart — the honest position is that nobody knows the right human dose of SR-15099, and taking more of an untested opioid carries its own risk.

Can You Verify What You Have?

Partly. Less than you can for most substances, and the gap is worth understanding before anything else on this page.

SR-15099 is assigned CAS 2134602-44-9. That number is well formed — CAS numbers carry a check digit, and this one computes correctly — and it sits immediately before SR-17018's 2134602-45-0, which is what you would expect of two compounds registered together from the same chemistry paper.

What you cannot do is follow that number anywhere public. Enter SR-17018's CAS into PubChem and it resolves at once to CID 130431397, with a formal chemical name and a government substance identifier attached. Enter SR-15099's and nothing comes back. Searching the name returns nothing either, in PubChem or in ChEMBL.

The structure named at the top of this page — SR-17018 with bromine in place of one chlorine — does exist in those databases, as CID 130431412, carrying nothing but database codes. Tying that structure to that CAS number, and both to the name SR-15099, needs paid access to the CAS registry itself. The identification is consistent with everything we can check, and it rests on records we cannot read.

The practical consequence is unchanged: no public certified reference standard exists for SR-15099. A testing laboratory can tell a customer what a sample appears to contain. It cannot match it against a certified sample of a compound for which none has been published.

Three further findings come from the mouse work (Acevedo-Canabal et al., 2023). SR-15099 behaves as a noncompetitive partial agonist in cells and in brainstem tissue, the same odd behavior SR-17018 shows. Given daily for a week it produced no escalating locomotor response, where fentanyl, morphine and oliceridine all sensitized — and brain and plasma levels on day seven matched day one, so nothing was accumulating. The compound used in those experiments was made at Scripps as a mesylate salt and checked by NMR at better than 95% purity, which is not a claim anyone can make about material bought online.

What People Are Reporting

SR-15099 only reached the general market around September 2026, so the entire human record is a few dozen self-reports from online communities. This is anecdote, not research: the people writing it have no way to verify what they took, nobody is tracking who had a bad time and stopped posting, and the reports contradict each other. It is included here because it is all that exists, and because a few patterns in it are worth knowing.

  • Reports split on effectiveness, most likely because of the potency difference above. Some describe it holding withdrawal off for 24 hours at a low dose; others describe needing to redose four times a day and still being in pain.
  • At least one report of precipitated-withdrawal-like symptoms after taking SR-15099 while on another opioid — sudden sweats, chills, and severe discomfort within about an hour. The lab data predict this should not happen. One report is not a pattern, but “it cannot precipitate withdrawal” is an inference from a single cell study, not a tested fact in humans.
  • Product quality is inconsistent. Reports describe chalky tablets that dissolve poorly and visibly misaligned score lines, which means the amount per tablet cannot be assumed to be what the label says, or even consistent within a batch.
  • Nobody has reported trying both compounds weeks apart, which is the comparison that would actually settle the question. Until someone does, treat every head-to-head claim as a guess.

Mechanism

Conventional opioids such as morphine and fentanyl activate the mu-opioid receptor and recruit both G proteins (which produce pain relief) and beta-arrestin-2 (linked in animal studies to respiratory depression, constipation, and tolerance). SR-15099 and SR-17018 favor the G-protein pathway and act as partial agonists: they produce a submaximal signal even at full receptor occupancy. Research from the Bohn lab, including a 2021 study in PNAS, found that this class of biased agonists produce sustained G-protein activation and behave as non-competitive agonists — meaning they do not simply compete with other opioids for the same binding site in the way a standard agonist or antagonist would.

You will often see it said that these compounds bind a separate, allosteric site on the receptor. That is a hypothesis offered to explain the non-competitive behavior, not a measured finding, and the direct structural evidence does not support it: a 2022 cryo-EM study resolved SR-17018 in the receptor's main (orthosteric) pocket, the same pocket morphine uses, contacting helix TM3 and barely touching TM6 and TM7. No allosteric site has been imaged. SR-15099 itself has never been resolved in a structure at all. What has been measured repeatedly is the behavior; the mechanism behind it remains an open question, and SR-15099's share of that behavior is inferred from one study.

Our full analysis of this mechanism — the structures, the explanations we tested and refuted, and what remains unmeasured — is on the receptor research page.

Research

In mice, SR-15099 produces robust analgesia with minimal hyperlocomotion, and the respiratory findings are mixed. A 2023 study on locomotor activity (Acevedo-Canabal et al., Biomolecules) found that both SR-17018 and SR-15099 caused only submaximal hyperactivity in mice, in contrast to morphine, fentanyl, and oliceridine, which were fully efficacious. All of this is animal work: rodent findings often do not translate to people, and no human studies exist. Whether SR-15099 shares SR-17018's reported ability to reduce opioid tolerance and ease withdrawal in humans has not been tested.

Key Papers

The 4 most relevant studies behind this page, each with a plain-English summary. Titles link to the paper; PubMed has the free abstract.

  1. Structural study Molecular recognition of morphine and fentanyl by the human μ-opioid receptor

    Zhuang et al. · 2022 · Cell 185:4361 · PubMed

    Imaged the receptor with five drugs bound, SR-17018 among them (PDB 8EFL). It found SR-17018 in the receptor's main pocket rather than a separate allosteric site, gripping a different part of it than conventional opioids do. Relevant here because it undercuts the common claim that this family works by binding somewhere else entirely. SR-15099 was not included.

  2. Animal study Bias factor and therapeutic window correlate to predict safer opioid analgesics

    Schmid et al. · 2017 · Cell · PubMed

    The Scripps paper that introduced this family of compounds (including SR-17018 and SR-15099). Across a series of opioids, the more a drug favored G-protein signaling over β-arrestin, the wider the gap in mice between the dose that relieved pain and the dose that slowed breathing.

  3. Animal study Hyperactivity in mice induced by opioid agonists with partial intrinsic efficacy and biased agonism administered alone and in combination with morphine

    Acevedo-Canabal et al. · 2023 · Biomolecules · PubMed

    The main study that tests SR-15099 directly. Morphine, fentanyl and oliceridine fully drove opioid-type hyperactivity in mice, while SR-15099 and SR-17018 produced only partial hyperactivity. Combining SR-17018 with morphine reduced hyperactivity and increased pain relief.

  4. Lab study G protein signaling-biased mu opioid receptor agonists that produce sustained G protein activation are noncompetitive agonists

    Stahl et al. · 2021 · PNAS · PubMed

    Explains what makes these compounds unusual: SR-17018 holds the mu-opioid receptor in a lasting signaling state and doesn't compete with other opioids for the same binding spot, yet naloxone-type antagonists can still reverse its effects in animals.

Effects

No human effect profile has been established. In animals, the reported profile is analgesia with little hyperactivity. The claim of little respiratory depression is weaker than it is usually presented: one group found no depression at the doses they tested, while another, dosing by mouth, found every opioid it tested depressed breathing dose-dependently, with similar effects at the highest doses (Hill et al., 2023). None of this should be read as a description of what a person would experience.

Harm Reduction

This is an untested opioid. Because no human safety data exist, treat every dose as an experiment with unknown risk. The absence of reported respiratory depression in mice does not mean it cannot cause it in people.

  • Verify your product. Research chemicals are frequently mislabeled or contaminated, and a compound sold as SR-15099 may be something else — including a far more potent opioid. Use fentanyl test strips and, where available, drug-checking services. A certificate of analysis from whoever sold it is not evidence — several sellers in this market have been caught posting fake or altered lab results. Mail-in and drop-off testing does not require your name or a return address; samples are matched to you by a code. The UNC Street Drug Analysis Lab accepts samples free through harm-reduction programmes in 45 states, and some university and non-profit services will too.
  • Tablet quality is unreliable. SR-15099 is being sold as pressed tablets, and users report chalky tablets that dissolve poorly with score lines that don't line up. That means you cannot assume a tablet contains what the label claims, or that halves are equal.
  • Do not mix it with other opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other depressants, which multiply the risk of dangerously slowed breathing.
  • Lowered tolerance may mean higher overdose risk. If SR-17018 lowers opioid tolerance in people as community reports suggest, a return to a former dose of a full opioid could be fatal. Treat yourself as opioid-naive after any break, and keep naloxone (Narcan) on hand.
  • Do not use it alone while its effects are unknown, and have someone who can call for help.
  • Talk to a clinician if you are trying to stop opioids. Approved treatments such as buprenorphine and methadone have decades of safety and effectiveness data that SR-15099 lacks.

If you or someone else may be overdosing, call emergency services and give naloxone if available. In the US, the Never Use Alone hotline is 800-484-3731, and SAMHSA's helpline is 1-800-662-4357.
