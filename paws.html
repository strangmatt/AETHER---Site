PAWS

Post-acute withdrawal syndrome — and psilocybin as a way to treat it

What PAWS Is

Withdrawal has two stages. The first is acute withdrawal: the days of sweating, shaking, vomiting, pain and insomnia that follow the last dose. It is physical, it is obvious, and it ends. The second stage is quieter and much longer. Post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS), also called protracted withdrawal, is the stretch of weeks to months afterward in which the body is no longer in withdrawal but the brain has not yet recovered.

People in PAWS are sober and feel terrible. Nothing is enjoyable, sleep is broken, anxiety comes in waves, and thinking is slow. From the outside it looks like depression or a lack of willpower. From the inside it feels like proof that this is what life without the drug will always be like. That belief is wrong, but it is persuasive, and the one thing guaranteed to stop the symptoms within minutes is the drug.

That is why PAWS matters: it is a main driver of relapse. Most people who relapse do not do it during the acute stage, when they are often in a detox unit. They do it weeks or months later, at home, during this stage, which most treatment programs barely mention.

Symptoms

A 2022 systematic review of 27 studies of post-acute alcohol withdrawal found that the syndrome is mostly one of negative mood. The same picture is described after opioids, benzodiazepines and stimulants.

  • Anhedonia. Food, music, sex and company feel flat. This is usually the symptom people find hardest.
  • Low mood and dysphoria. A grey, restless unhappiness that does not need a reason.
  • Anxiety and irritability. Often arriving in waves, and often out of proportion to what set them off.
  • Sleep disturbance. Trouble falling asleep, waking through the night, vivid dreams, and exhaustion during the day.
  • Cognitive fog. Poor concentration, poor short-term memory and slow thinking.
  • Craving. Sometimes constant, more often set off by stress or by the places and people tied to using.
  • Stress sensitivity. Ordinary problems feel like emergencies.

The symptoms come and go. People describe good days followed by a bad stretch with no clear cause, which is discouraging if nobody has warned you to expect it. The overall trend is still improvement.

How Long It Lasts

There is no agreed timeline, and it differs by drug and by person. The alcohol review found that symptoms develop in early abstinence and can persist for four to six months or longer. Reports after long-term opioid or benzodiazepine use describe a year or more for some people. Heavier and longer use generally means a longer recovery.

The important fact is that PAWS ends. The brain changes behind it are adaptations, and adaptations reverse.

Why It Happens

Repeated drug use pushes the brain's reward and stress systems far from their normal settings, and the brain pushes back. It turns down its own reward signalling and turns up its stress signalling so that it can function with the drug present. When the drug is removed, those compensations are still in place: too little reward, too much stress. The addiction researchers George Koob and Nora Volkow call this the withdrawal and negative-affect stage of addiction, and it is the part that keeps people using to feel normal instead of to feel good.

The alcohol review linked PAWS symptoms to measurable differences in stress hormones, serotonin and orexin, and to changes in the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex. These are the regions that handle motivation and self-control.

PAWS is not an official diagnosis. It is not in the DSM-5, and researchers have argued since the 1990s about whether it should be. The 2022 review concluded there is credible evidence the syndrome is real, and that it still lacks agreed criteria and good treatment trials. This is a large part of why so few people in recovery are ever told about it.

What Helps Now

No medication is approved for PAWS. What exists is a set of treatments with better evidence for the underlying disorder, plus the basics.

  • Medication for opioid use disorder. Buprenorphine and methadone are the best-supported treatments in addiction medicine. In a pooled analysis of cohort studies, people in treatment with either one died at a fraction of the rate of people out of treatment. They also prevent most opioid PAWS, because the opioid system is stabilized instead of abruptly emptied. Being on one of them is recovery, not a failure of it.
  • Medication for alcohol use disorder. Naltrexone and acamprosate reduce relapse. Some prescribers use gabapentin for sleep and anxiety in the months after alcohol detox. The evidence for treating PAWS symptoms directly is thin.
  • Treating depression and anxiety. If low mood persists, it deserves treatment in its own right, whatever is causing it.
  • Sleep, exercise and routine. Regular aerobic exercise is the cheapest thing known to improve mood, sleep and craving together.
  • People. Peer support, counselling and housing all lower relapse risk. Isolation raises it.
  • Knowing what it is. People who have been told that PAWS exists, and that it ends, are less likely to read a bad week as a permanent state.

Why Psychedelics Might Help

PAWS is a state in which the brain is stuck in a low-reward, high-stress pattern and the mind is stuck in the conclusions that go with it. Classic psychedelics act on both, which is the argument for studying them here.

  • Neuroplasticity. In laboratory studies, psilocybin, LSD and DMT make cortical neurons grow new branches and connections. In mice, a single dose of psilocybin produced new dendritic spines in the frontal cortex within 24 hours, and a share of them were still there a month later. The frontal cortex is one of the regions PAWS impairs.
  • Mood. In randomized trials, one full dose of psilocybin with psychological support reduced depression for weeks. Low mood and anhedonia are the core of PAWS.
  • Loosening fixed patterns. Craving is a learned response, and so is the belief that sober life will always feel this bad. People in psilocybin addiction trials often describe the session as the point where those patterns stopped feeling inevitable.
  • Not habit-forming. Classic psychedelics do not produce compulsive use or physical dependence, and tolerance builds within days. This is unusual for a drug that changes mood this much.

Each of these is a reason to run trials. None of them is proof that it works.

What the Research Shows

No trial has tested psilocybin, or any psychedelic, for PAWS itself. The evidence below comes from nearby questions: depression, and substance use disorders in people who were mostly past detox. It is encouraging and it is early.

The closest study to PAWS. A French pilot trial enrolled 30 people with severe alcohol use disorder and depression who had finished detox 14 to 60 days earlier, which is the post-acute window. Twenty received two 25 mg psilocybin sessions with therapy and ten received a 1 mg dose. Craving was lower after the full dose. The trial was built to test feasibility, so it is a signal and not a result.

Alcohol. In a trial of 93 people, two psilocybin sessions with therapy cut heavy drinking days to about 10% over 32 weeks, against 24% in the placebo group.

Tobacco and cocaine. In a 2026 smoking trial, 40.5% of people given one psilocybin dose with CBT were verified abstinent at six months, against 10% on the nicotine patch with CBT. In a 2026 cocaine trial, people given psilocybin had more abstinent days through six months than those given placebo.

Depression. Two large randomized trials found that a single 25 mg dose with psychological support reduced depression symptoms over the following weeks.

Opioids. There is no completed randomized trial. What exists is survey data. In a national US sample of 214,505 adults, lifetime psilocybin use was associated with 30% lower odds of opioid use disorder, and no other drug showed that pattern. An earlier analysis of 44,000 people who had used illicit opioids found a 27% lower risk of opioid dependence among those who had used psychedelics. In an online survey of 444 people who credited a psychedelic experience with reducing their drug use, 96% had met criteria for a substance use disorder beforehand and 27% did afterward. Surveys like these cannot show cause, and the last one only recruited people who had improved.

Limits. Every trial above used a full dose in a clinic, with screening, preparation and therapy. None used small doses. None followed people for years. Not every psilocybin trial has been positive, and none is yet a large phase 3 study. Trials also exclude the people at highest risk, including anyone with a history of psychosis or bipolar disorder.

The Founder's Account

ÆTHER exists because of this subject. This is the founder's own account, and it is one person's experience.

After ten years of relapsing, I finally understood why: untreated post-acute withdrawal syndrome kept dragging me back. Just out of rehab and living in transitional housing, I started mini-dosing psilocybin. Within a day, my PAWS symptoms were gone, and my depression went with them. That was six years ago, and I’ve been depression- and SUD-free ever since.

A single account cannot separate the drug from expectation, timing and everything else that changes when someone leaves rehab. The controlled studies of small doses in healthy volunteers have mostly found that placebo does as well, and none of them enrolled people in PAWS. The account is here because it is the reason this page was written, and because the question it raises has not been tested. See the microdosing guide for the small-dose research in full.

Risks Specific to Recovery

The general risks of psilocybin are on the psilocybin page. These are the ones that matter most to someone in early recovery.

  • Never during acute withdrawal. Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause seizures and death, and they need medical care. Opioid withdrawal is rarely fatal but is physically punishing. A psychedelic treats none of this and makes the experience worse. Everything on this page is about the stage that comes after.
  • Your tolerance is gone. After weeks of abstinence, a dose of opioids you used to take routinely can kill you. This is the biggest danger of the PAWS period, and no psychedelic changes it. Keep naloxone, and make sure the people around you know where it is.
  • Do not stop a medication to make room for a psychedelic. Leaving buprenorphine or methadone treatment is followed by a sharp rise in overdose deaths. Psilocybin has no known dangerous interaction with either, though the combination has barely been studied.
  • Lithium and tramadol. Lithium combined with psychedelics carries a documented seizure risk and should be avoided outright. Tramadol also lowers the seizure threshold. SSRIs and SNRIs usually blunt the effect, and the common mistake is to take more.
  • Psychosis and bipolar disorder. A personal or family history of schizophrenia, psychosis or bipolar disorder is a reason not to take psychedelics at any dose.
  • A hard experience in a fragile state. Early recovery is a period of high anxiety and, for some people, suicidal thinking. A difficult session can make both worse for days. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 in the US.
  • It is not a replacement for treatment. The trials added psilocybin to therapy and standard care. Nobody in them was told to stop everything else.
  • Legal exposure. A possession charge can cost housing, probation or a treatment placement, which are the things that keep people stable in early recovery.

If You Are Considering It

Practical harm reduction, on the assumption the decision is already made.

  • Wait until acute withdrawal is fully over and you are medically stable. For alcohol and benzodiazepines, that means after a supervised detox.
  • Keep your treatment. Stay on prescribed medication, keep going to appointments, and tell your prescriber if you can. Many will not approve. They still need to know what you are taking.
  • Start low. The microdosing guide covers small-dose ranges, schedules and how to measure them. The psilocybin page covers full doses.
  • For a full dose, have a sober sitter you trust, a safe place, and nothing you have to do the next day. The crisis guide is written for sitters.
  • Plan the weeks after. In the trials, the session opened a window and the therapy around it did the work. Line up a counsellor, a peer group or one person you will talk to honestly.
  • Keep a daily log of mood, sleep and craving, starting before you begin. It is the only way to tell a real change from a hoped-for one.
  • Set a stopping rule. If anxiety, sleep or craving get worse, stop. Do not raise the dose.

Legal, supervised options exist. Oregon and Colorado license psilocybin services for adults without a diagnosis, and clinical trials for substance use disorders are recruiting at ClinicalTrials.gov.

Key Papers

The 14 studies behind this page, each with a plain-English summary. Titles link to the paper; PubMed has the free abstract.

  1. Systematic review Neurobiology and symptomatology of post-acute alcohol withdrawal: a mixed-studies systematic review

    Bahji, Crockford & el-Guebaly · 2022 · Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs 83:461 · PubMed

    The best single summary of PAWS. Across 27 studies, the syndrome was mostly negative mood beginning in early abstinence and lasting four to six months or longer, with anxiety, anhedonia, poor sleep, cognitive problems, craving and irritability. The symptoms predicted a return to drinking. The authors concluded the concept is credible and that it still needs formal criteria and proper treatment trials.

  2. Review Should protracted withdrawal from drugs be included in DSM-IV?

    Satel et al. · 1993 · American Journal of Psychiatry 150:695 · PubMed

    The paper that framed the diagnostic argument. It reviewed protracted withdrawal across alcohol, opioids and other drugs and weighed whether it belonged in the diagnostic manual. It was left out, and it remains out, which is why PAWS has no official definition.

  3. Review Neurobiology of addiction: a neurocircuitry analysis

    Koob & Volkow · 2016 · The Lancet Psychiatry 3:760 · PubMed

    The standard model of addiction as a three-stage cycle. Its account of the withdrawal and negative-affect stage, with reward systems turned down and stress systems turned up, is the best available explanation of why PAWS feels the way it does.

  4. Meta-analysis Mortality risk during and after opioid substitution treatment: systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies

    Sordo et al. · 2017 · BMJ 357:j1550 · PubMed

    Why nobody should stop methadone or buprenorphine to try something else. Across cohort studies, death rates were substantially lower in treatment than out of it, and the weeks just after leaving treatment were the most dangerous.

  5. Pilot trial Psilocybin in alcohol use disorder and comorbid depressive symptoms: results from a feasibility randomized clinical trial

    Luquiens et al. · 2025 · Addiction · PubMed

    The closest any trial has come to PAWS. Thirty people with severe alcohol use disorder and depression, 14 to 60 days out of detox, received two sessions of 25 mg or 1 mg psilocybin with therapy. Craving was lower after the full dose. It was a feasibility study, and blinding was a problem: several people in the low-dose group guessed their assignment.

  6. Randomized trial Percentage of heavy drinking days following psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy vs placebo in the treatment of adult patients with alcohol use disorder

    Bogenschutz et al. · 2022 · JAMA Psychiatry 79:953 · PubMed

    The largest psilocybin addiction trial with published long-term results. Two sessions with twelve weeks of therapy cut heavy drinking days to about 10% over 32 weeks, against 24% with an active placebo.

  7. Randomized trial Psilocybin or nicotine patch for smoking cessation: a pilot randomized clinical trial

    Johnson et al. · 2026 · JAMA Network Open 9:e260972 · PubMed

    82 smokers received either one high dose of psilocybin or the nicotine patch, each with 13 weeks of CBT. At six months, 40.5% of the psilocybin group were biochemically verified abstinent, against 10% on the patch. Nobody was blinded, so expectation could account for part of the difference.

  8. Randomized trial Psilocybin in the treatment of cocaine use disorder

    Hendricks et al. · 2026 · JAMA Network Open 9:e2611029 · PubMed

    The first randomized psilocybin trial in cocaine use disorder, a condition with no approved medication. People given psilocybin had more abstinent days through six months than those given placebo.

  9. Randomized trial Single-dose psilocybin treatment for major depressive disorder

    Raison et al. · 2023 · JAMA 330:843 · PubMed

    One 25 mg dose with psychological support, against an active placebo, in people with major depression. Depression scores fell further in the psilocybin group over six weeks.

  10. Randomized trial Single-dose psilocybin for a treatment-resistant episode of major depression

    Goodwin et al. · 2022 · New England Journal of Medicine 387:1637 · PubMed

    The largest psilocybin depression trial so far. A single 25 mg dose reduced depression at three weeks more than a 1 mg dose did. The benefit had faded for many participants by twelve weeks, and adverse events, including suicidal thoughts, occurred in all groups.

  11. Survey Associations between classic psychedelics and opioid use disorder in a nationally-representative U.S. adult sample

    Jones et al. · 2022 · Scientific Reports 12:4099 · PubMed

    Among 214,505 US adults, lifetime psilocybin use was associated with 30% lower odds of opioid use disorder. No other substance, including other psychedelics, showed the same association. It is a correlation, and the authors say trials are needed to find out whether it is causal.

  12. Survey The association of psychedelic use and opioid use disorders among illicit users in the United States

    Pisano et al. · 2017 · Journal of Psychopharmacology 31:606 · PubMed

    The earlier analysis that the 2022 study set out to replicate. Among 44,000 people who had used illicit opioids, psychedelic use was associated with a 27% lower risk of opioid dependence and a 40% lower risk of opioid abuse in the past year.

  13. Survey Persisting reductions in cannabis, opioid, and stimulant misuse after naturalistic psychedelic use: an online survey

    Garcia-Romeu et al. · 2020 · Frontiers in Psychiatry 10:955 · PubMed

    444 people who said a psychedelic experience outside a clinic led them to cut down or quit another drug. Before it, 96% met criteria for a substance use disorder; afterward, 27% did. Larger doses and more meaningful experiences went with larger reductions. The survey only reached people with a success to report.

  14. Laboratory Psilocybin induces rapid and persistent growth of dendritic spines in frontal cortex in vivo

    Shao et al. · 2021 · Neuron 109:2535 · PubMed

    The clearest picture of the plasticity claim. One dose of psilocybin in mice increased the number and size of dendritic spines in the frontal cortex within a day, and some of the new connections lasted a month. It builds on Ly et al. (2018, Cell Reports), which showed the same growth in cultured neurons for several psychedelics. Both are animal and cell studies.

Legal Status

Psilocybin is Schedule I under US federal law. Ann Arbor decriminalized natural psychedelics in 2020, Oregon licenses supervised psilocybin services, and Colorado has a regulated framework under Proposition 122. Decriminalization changes enforcement priority, not legality, and none of it covers buying, selling or mailing. Anyone on probation, parole or in court-ordered treatment faces consequences beyond the charge itself. Know your local laws.
