SR and Buprenorphine

Nearly the same mechanism. Very different evidence.

Most people who buy SR-17018 or SR-15099 are trying to get off something else. That is the whole reason this class has a following: the mouse studies showed it reversing morphine tolerance and suppressing withdrawal, and word spread from there.

There is already a medicine for that job, and the two work through nearly the same receptor mechanism — with one documented difference that may matter a great deal. Every claim below carries its source.

The Shared Mechanism

Buprenorphine, the active half of Suboxone, is described in the pharmacology literature as having "high affinity, low intrinsic activity and slow dissociation" at the µ-opioid receptor (Johnson & McCagh, 2000). That is a partial agonist that grips hard and lets go slowly.

Now read the SR profile. Partial agonist, topping out at 75% of a full agonist's response in the assay where both SR compounds were measured side by side. Long-acting, with slower absorption than morphine. Hard to displace in binding assays. And when researchers tracked how long the receptor stayed chemically marked after the drug was washed away, SR-17018's signal persisted for hours instead of minutes — a result they found in only one other compound they tested. That compound was buprenorphine (Fritzwanker et al., 2021).

The properties people cite as making SR-17018 promising for getting off opioids are, nearly item for item, the properties that make buprenorphine work. The difference is not the mechanism. It is forty years of human data.

Our full analysis of this mechanism — the structures, the explanations we tested and refuted, and what remains unmeasured — is on the receptor research page.

Where They Diverge

The mechanism is not identical. The group that made SR-17018 ran the comparison themselves and wrote it into their abstract.

"Substitution with SR-17018 in morphine-tolerant mice restores morphine potency and efficacy, whereas the onset of opioid withdrawal is prevented. This is in contrast to buprenorphine, which can suppress withdrawal, but produces and maintains morphine antinociceptive tolerance."
— Grim et al., 2020

It appears to unwind tolerance rather than hold it in place. Mice given SR-17018 long term developed no antinociceptive tolerance, no receptor desensitization in the periaqueductal gray, and no supersensitised adenylyl cyclase in the striatum. Those last two are not side issues — they are the cellular adaptations that constitute dependence. Buprenorphine suppresses withdrawal while leaving tolerance intact. On this evidence SR-17018 does something different in kind: it substitutes for the drug and appears to reverse the adaptation underneath it.

It does not compete. From the follow-up work: "partial agonists of this class do not compete with full agonist activation" (Stahl et al., 2021). Buprenorphine's defining clinical problem is that it does compete. Its high affinity throws whatever is already on the receptor off, which is precipitated withdrawal, and that fear is the single biggest reason people refuse it. A compound that occupies the receptor without displacing anything could in principle be started on top of an existing habit without that reaction.

If both of those hold up in people, they would explain why users report SR working better than Suboxone in ways that have nothing to do with potency: it can be started without a day of engineered agony, and it may leave the receptor system better off rather than merely stable.

The cost. Non-competition cuts both ways. Part of why buprenorphine keeps people alive is blockade: while it is on board, heroin or fentanyl taken on top does much less, because it cannot get to the receptor. SR-17018 gives that protection up by design. Nothing stops a full agonist taken alongside it, and taking a full agonist on top of something is the circumstance in which people die. The property that makes it pleasant to start is the same property that removes the safety net.

Two further cautions on the evidence itself. All of it is mice, hot-plate testing, from the laboratory that invented the compound and holds a patent on it — which does not make it wrong, and does mean it needs replicating elsewhere. And none of it has been weighed against the outcome that matters, because nobody has counted how many people taking SR-17018 are alive a year later.

Mortality

A meta-analysis pooled 19 cohorts following 122,885 people on methadone and 15,831 on buprenorphine (Sordo et al., BMJ 2017). For buprenorphine, all-cause mortality ran at 4.3 deaths per 1,000 person-years during treatment and 9.5 after leaving it. For methadone, 11.3 against 36.1.

Being in treatment roughly halves the death rate on buprenorphine and cuts it threefold on methadone. The weeks right after stopping are the most dangerous of all, which is the reverse of how most people think about "getting clean".

No compound sold as a research chemical has a number like this, ours included. It is not that the evidence is weaker. There is none.

Common Objections

The reasons people give for refusing buprenorphine are mostly real. Each is answerable.

  • Precipitated withdrawal. The big one. Take buprenorphine while a full agonist still occupies your receptors and it knocks that drug off, dropping you into violent withdrawal within minutes. Researchers interviewing people who inject drugs found this fear was the main thing shaping how they saw buprenorphine, sharpened by fentanyl saturating the supply (2026 interview study). The fear is justified. It is also manageable — low-dose initiation protocols exist precisely for people coming off fentanyl, and emergency medicine has published guidance on preventing and treating it (Spadaro et al., 2022). Ask specifically about low-dose or "micro" induction.
  • The naloxone in Suboxone. Widely misunderstood. It is there to deter injection, is barely absorbed under the tongue, and does essentially nothing when the tablet or film is taken as directed. It does not make the medicine weaker. Plain buprenorphine without naloxone also exists if it remains a problem.
  • "It's just swapping one drug for another." This framing costs lives. The mortality figures above are what swapping looks like. There is no treatment-free version of those numbers.
  • Access and stigma. Genuine obstacles, and smaller than they were. Since 2023 any US clinician with a DEA registration can prescribe it; no special waiver is needed. Telehealth prescribing exists. SAMHSA's helpline, 1-800-662-4357, is free, confidential and open all day and night.
  • Previous bad experience. Often a dose set too low, or an induction attempted too early. Both are fixable, and worth raising with a prescriber rather than abandoning the approach.

If You Are Using SR to Get Off Something

  • Talk to a prescriber about buprenorphine first. It is the same mechanistic idea with evidence behind it, a known dose, and a pharmacy supply chain.
  • Do not combine them casually. Both are partial agonists with high affinity. Either could displace the other and precipitate withdrawal, and nobody has studied the interaction in either direction.
  • Do not stop methadone or buprenorphine to try SR. Leaving treatment is the most dangerous period in the data above.
  • Keep naloxone within reach whatever you are taking, and make sure someone else knows where it is. See the crisis guide.
  • If the pull is post-acute withdrawal — the months of flat mood and broken sleep after detox — that is a different problem with its own page. See PAWS.

Key Papers

  1. Meta-analysis Mortality risk during and after opioid substitution treatment

    Sordo et al. · 2017 · BMJ 357:j1550 · PubMed

    The mortality figures above. Nineteen cohorts, 138,000 people. In treatment versus out of it, and the sharp spike in risk immediately after leaving.

  2. Receptor pharmacology SR-17018 stimulates atypical µ-opioid receptor phosphorylation and dephosphorylation

    Fritzwanker et al. · 2021 · Molecules 26:4509 · PubMed

    The paper that ties the two together. SR-17018 leaves the receptor chemically marked for hours after washout where a full agonist's mark clears in minutes, and the only other drug tested that behaved that way was buprenorphine. Naloxone in the washout reversed it for both.

  3. Animal study A G protein signaling-biased agonist at the µ-opioid receptor reverses morphine tolerance while preventing morphine withdrawal

    Grim et al. · 2020 · Neuropsychopharmacology 45:416 · PubMed

    Where the divergence is documented. Chronic SR-17018 produced no tolerance and none of the cellular adaptations that go with it, and switching morphine-tolerant mice onto it restored morphine's potency while heading off withdrawal. The authors contrast this directly with buprenorphine, which suppresses withdrawal but keeps tolerance in place. Mice, one laboratory, and that laboratory holds the patent.

  4. Pharmacology review Buprenorphine and naloxone for heroin dependence

    Johnson & McCagh · 2000 · Current Psychiatry Reports 2:519 · PubMed

    Why the combination exists: high affinity, low intrinsic activity and slow dissociation give safety and flexible dosing, and the naloxone is included to deter injection rather than to add any effect when taken as directed.

  5. Clinical review Buprenorphine precipitated opioid withdrawal: prevention and management in the ED setting

    Spadaro et al. · 2022 · American Journal of Emergency Medicine 58:22 · PubMed

    What to do about the thing people are most afraid of. Written for emergency clinicians, and the reason it is worth asking a prescriber about low-dose initiation rather than ruling buprenorphine out.

  6. Volunteer study Mechanism-based PK/PD modeling of the respiratory depressant effect of buprenorphine and fentanyl in healthy volunteers

    Yassen et al. · 2007 · Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics 81:50 · PubMed

    Where the ceiling on buprenorphine's effect on breathing was characterized in people, measured against fentanyl in the same framework. The kind of study the SR compounds have never had.
