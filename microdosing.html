Microdosing

Sub-perceptual doses — the practice, the evidence, and the risks

What Microdosing Means

A microdose is a dose small enough that it does not produce a psychedelic experience. There are no visuals, no ego dissolution, no impairment you would notice as being on a drug — the common rule of thumb is roughly 5–10% of a recreational dose, taken on a repeating schedule over weeks or months rather than once. People microdose with the intention of improving mood, focus, creativity or energy while continuing to work, drive and parent normally.

The word was popularized by psychologist James Fadiman, whose 2011 book The Psychedelic Explorer's Guide collected self-reports from people taking 10 µg of LSD every third day. The practice spread through Silicon Valley and then widely online, and by the late 2010s it had become the most common form of psychedelic use in some survey populations — well ahead of the research that could speak to whether it works.

That gap is the most important thing on this page. Microdosing is one of the few areas of psychedelics where the enthusiastic reports and the controlled evidence point in clearly different directions. The section on what the research found is not a formality — read it before anything else here.

Common Dose Ranges

These are the ranges reported in surveys and used in trials, given for reference, not as recommendations. The target is below the threshold of noticeable effect: if you can tell you are on something, it is not a microdose, and people who feel it are usually above the range rather than unusually sensitive.

The measurement problem is the practical heart of microdosing. A microdose is small enough that ordinary methods cannot divide it accurately. Mushroom potency varies between species, between individual mushrooms, and between the cap and the stem of one mushroom, so a 0.1 g piece broken off a cap is a guess — grinding a whole batch to an even powder and weighing from that on a milligram scale is the only way to make consecutive doses comparable. LSD cannot be divided by cutting a tab, because the drug is not distributed evenly across the blotter; volumetric dosing (dissolving a known tab in a known volume of alcohol or distilled water, refrigerated and dark, then measuring by syringe) is the standard approach. A kitchen scale cannot weigh a 2 mg dose of anything.

DMT and 5-MeO-DMT are not microdosed in any meaningful sense, for reasons covered on their pages.

Schedules

Tolerance to psychedelics builds fast and clears over a few days, so no protocol doses daily. Every schedule in common use is built around that, and the differences between them are a matter of convention rather than evidence — none has been shown superior to any other.

  • The Fadiman protocol — one day on, two days off, repeating. The most widely used. Day 1 is the dose, day 2 is the "afterglow" people often report, day 3 is a clean baseline.
  • The Stamets protocol — four days on, three days off, usually with psilocybin combined with lion's mane mushroom and niacin. Popularized by mycologist Paul Stamets on a neurogenesis rationale that has not been tested in people.
  • Two fixed days a week — for example Monday and Thursday. Used in several of the clinical trials because it is easy to blind and easy to adhere to.
  • Time-limited courses. Most protocols run four to eight weeks and then stop for a break of similar length. Open-ended daily-ish use over many months is where the safety questions below get sharper, and nobody has studied it.

What the Research Actually Found

The evidence splits cleanly in two, and which half you read determines what you conclude.

Surveys and observational studies are strongly positive. In the largest, 4,050 microdosers scored lower on depression, anxiety and stress than 4,653 people who did not microdose (Rootman et al., 2021). Survey after survey finds the same thing: most people who microdose say it helps, and they say so consistently.

Placebo-controlled studies are not. When the comparison is against a placebo rather than against non-users, the benefit mostly disappears:

  • The self-blinding study (Szigeti et al., 2021) is the cleverest design in this literature. 191 people who were already microdosing prepared their own capsules — some real, some placebo — following instructions that let them randomize and blind themselves at home, so nobody had to be recruited into a lab. Everyone improved: well-being, mood and life satisfaction all rose significantly over four weeks. The placebo group improved just as much. What predicted feeling better was not whether you had taken the drug but whether you believed you had.
  • The psilocybin crossover trial (Cavanna et al., 2022) gave 34 new microdosers 0.5 g of dried mushrooms or a matched placebo, double-blind, on two days a week. Acute effects were stronger on the real dose — but only among participants who correctly guessed which week they were in. Across every other measure there was no effect, apart from a few small shifts in the direction of worse cognitive performance.
  • The LSD dose-response studies (Bershad et al., 2019; de Wit et al., 2022) gave healthy volunteers placebo, 6.5, 13 or 26 µg in the lab. Effects were dose-related and detectable, and 13 µg raised ratings of vigour. Mood, cognition and most physiological measures were unchanged; blood pressure rose at the higher doses. Repeating 13 and 26 µg doses in 56 people produced negligible changes in mood or cognition.
  • The reviews agree. A 2026 meta-analysis of 14 studies (1,614 participants) found no cognitive benefit at all — a pooled effect of essentially zero. A 2026 systematic review of 24 studies concluded that controlled trials show real subjective and brain-activity effects but minimal impact on cognition, creativity or sustained mood.

How to hold both halves at once. The honest reading is not that microdosers are lying or imagining things — people in these studies genuinely felt better. It is that the improvement does not appear to come from the drug. Microdosing is unusually well set up to produce a placebo response: you have chosen to do it, paid for it, built a ritual and a schedule around it, told yourself you are working on yourself, and you are checking in on your own mood daily while expecting it to improve. Those are the ingredients of a strong expectancy effect, and the self-blinding study measured exactly that. Placebo effects on mood are real effects — you are not imagining the benefit — but you would get them from the ritual without the drug, and the drug is where the legal and medical risk lives.

One genuine limitation cuts the other way: nearly all controlled trials enrolled healthy volunteers over a few weeks. It remains possible that microdosing helps people with depression or ADHD in ways short studies of healthy people could not detect. Trials in patients with major depression are underway. Until they report, that is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Risks and Who Should Not

Microdoses are small, and the acute risk is correspondingly small. The concerns here are about repetition — taking a serotonergic drug dozens or hundreds of times is a different exposure from taking one twice a year, and it is the part no study has followed for long enough.

  • Heart valves — the most substantive unknown. Psychedelics activate the 5-HT2B receptor, and chronic 5-HT2B activation is the established mechanism by which fen-phen and the ergot drug cabergoline caused valvular heart disease. A 2023 pharmacological analysis (Tagen et al.) found that LSD, psilocin, DMT and MDMA all bind 5-HT2B as a partial agonist with potency equal to or greater than their binding at 5-HT2A, the target responsible for the psychedelic effect — psilocin especially so. The authors' conclusion was that valvular heart disease is a plausible risk of chronic microdosing and that more work is needed to size it. No case has been linked to microdosing, and a later mouse study of prolonged low-dose LSD found no valve changes. This is a theoretical risk taken seriously by pharmacologists rather than a documented harm, and it argues for time-limited courses with real breaks rather than years of continuous use.
  • SSRIs and other serotonergic medication. Combining chronically dosed serotonergic psychedelics with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, lithium, tramadol or triptans is where interaction risk concentrates. Lithium with psychedelics carries a documented seizure risk and should be avoided outright. SSRIs usually blunt psychedelic effects rather than causing harm, which creates a particular trap: people respond by taking more.
  • Psychosis and bipolar risk. Anyone with a personal or family history of schizophrenia, psychotic illness or bipolar disorder should not microdose. Repeated dosing offers repeated opportunities for a destabilizing episode, and this exclusion applies to every protocol regardless of dose.
  • Anxiety and sleep. Survey reports of unwanted effects are led by increased anxiety, restlessness and disrupted sleep, especially at the upper end of the range. If a schedule makes you more anxious rather than less, that is the common adverse effect, not a sign you need to adjust the dose upward.
  • Driving and work. "Sub-perceptual" is a claim about what you notice, not a measurement of performance — and the one trial that looked found small shifts toward worse cognition, not better. Do not treat a dosing day as equivalent to a clean day for driving, operating machinery or safety-critical work.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. No data. Don't.
  • Legal exposure is cumulative. A microdose of a Schedule I substance is the same offence as a full dose, and a microdosing habit means possessing a supply, repeatedly, often at work or in a car. The quantity on hand, not the size of each dose, is what determines charge severity.

If You Are Going to Do It

Practical harm reduction, on the assumption the decision is already made.

  • Test what you have. Reagent kits confirm a substance class; sheets sold as LSD are a common vehicle for NBOMes and other substituted phenethylamines, which are active at microdose-scale amounts and have killed people. 2C-B sold loose is frequently something else. Fentanyl test strips do not apply to psychedelics but cost nothing to use on anything powdered.
  • Make doses from one homogenized batch — ground mushroom powder weighed on a milligram scale, or a volumetric LSD solution — so your doses are comparable to each other. Otherwise you are not running a schedule, you are taking a different drug amount each time.
  • Start at the bottom of the range and hold it for at least two weeks before changing anything. People who chase an effect upward end up lightly tripping at work.
  • Set an end date before you start. Four to eight weeks, then a break of comparable length. The open-ended version is the one with the unanswered safety question.
  • Keep a short daily log — mood, sleep, anxiety, one line — including on off days and through the break. Rated honestly, this is the only way to tell a real change from an expected one, and it is the same instrument the research used.
  • Consider running your own placebo control. The self-blinding method from the Szigeti study is public, free and designed for exactly this. If you want to know whether it is working for you rather than whether you believe it is, that is how to find out.
  • Tell your prescriber if you take any psychiatric or serotonergic medication, and don't stop a prescribed medication in order to microdose.

Microdosing and PAWS

One use that falls outside everything above is post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS) — the months of low mood, poor sleep, anhedonia and craving that can follow acute opioid or alcohol withdrawal and that drive a lot of relapse. The microdosing trials on this page all enrolled healthy volunteers, so none of them speak to it either way.

Two cautions carry over from the rest of this page. Psychedelics are not for acute withdrawal, which is a different and sometimes dangerous situation. And a single person's experience is exactly the kind of evidence the placebo-controlled studies above were built to test — it is a reason to look into something, not a reason to expect it will work for you.

Key Papers

The 8 studies behind this page, each with a plain-English summary. Titles link to the paper; PubMed has the free abstract.

  1. Self-blinded trial Self-blinding citizen science to explore psychedelic microdosing

    Szigeti et al. · 2021 · eLife 10:e62878 · PubMed

    The most important study on this page, and the largest placebo-controlled one. 191 people already microdosing were taught to randomize and blind themselves at home using their own supply. All groups improved substantially on mood, well-being and life satisfaction — and the placebo group improved as much as the microdosing group. What predicted improvement was believing you had taken a dose.

  2. Randomized crossover Microdosing with psilocybin mushrooms: a double-blind placebo-controlled study

    Cavanna et al. · 2022 · Translational Psychiatry 12:307 · PubMed

    34 people new to microdosing took 0.5 g of dried mushrooms or matched placebo twice a week, with a third party handling the blinding. Acute effects were stronger on the real dose only among people who correctly guessed their condition, alongside a measurable EEG change. Everything else showed no benefit, and a few measures moved slightly toward worse cognition.

  3. Randomized trial Acute subjective and behavioral effects of microdoses of lysergic acid diethylamide in healthy human volunteers

    Bershad et al. · 2019 · Biological Psychiatry 86:792 · PubMed

    The first properly blinded LSD microdosing experiment. 20 volunteers received placebo, 6.5, 13 and 26 µg in random order across four sessions. Subjective effects tracked dose and the highest dose raised vigour, but mood, cognition and most physiological measures were unaffected. The authors nominated 13 µg as a safe dose for repeated-dosing research.

  4. Randomized trial Repeated low doses of LSD in healthy adults: a placebo-controlled, dose-response study

    de Wit et al. · 2022 · Addiction Biology 27:e13143 · PubMed

    The follow-up that tested repetition rather than a single dose: 56 people took 13 or 26 µg of LSD, or placebo, four times. Changes in mood and cognition were negligible. It is the closest thing to a controlled test of an actual microdosing schedule.

  5. Pharmacology The risk of chronic psychedelic and MDMA microdosing for valvular heart disease

    Tagen et al. · 2023 · Journal of Psychopharmacology 37:876 · PubMed

    The main safety argument against long-term microdosing. Chronic activation of the 5-HT2B receptor is how fen-phen damaged heart valves; this analysis found LSD, psilocin, DMT and MDMA all hit 5-HT2B as partial agonists at least as potently as they hit the receptor that produces the psychedelic effect. No case has been documented in a microdoser, and the authors call it a potential risk needing study rather than a proven one.

  6. Survey Adults who microdose psychedelics report health related motivations and lower levels of anxiety and depression compared to non-microdosers

    Rootman et al. · 2021 · Scientific Reports 11:22479 · PubMed

    The largest observational study, and the strongest case for microdosing: 4,050 microdosers reported less depression, anxiety and stress than 4,653 non-microdosers. Because people chose for themselves whether to microdose, it cannot separate the drug from the kind of person who takes it, which is exactly what the placebo-controlled studies went on to test.

  7. Survey A systematic study of microdosing psychedelics

    Polito & Stevenson · 2019 · PLOS ONE 14:e0211023 · PubMed

    Tracked 98 microdosers daily through their own schedules. Found improvements on dosing days in several measures and a drop in mind-wandering, but also higher neuroticism in some participants — and, importantly, that expectation predicted outcomes. An early signal that this literature had a placebo problem.

  8. Meta-analysis Effects of psychedelic microdosing on cognitive functions: a systematic review and meta-analysis

    Pinhas et al. · 2026 · Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews 180:106493 · PubMed

    Pooled 14 studies and 1,614 participants to test the most common claim made for microdosing — that it sharpens thinking. The combined effect was essentially zero. Whatever microdosing does, improving cognitive performance in healthy people is not it.

Legal Status

A microdose carries the same legal status as a full dose. Psilocybin, LSD, mescaline, DMT and 2C-B are all Schedule I under US federal law, and no jurisdiction has carved out an exception for small amounts. Ann Arbor decriminalized natural psychedelics in 2020, Oregon licenses supervised psilocybin services (which are full-dose sessions, not microdosing), and Colorado has a regulated framework under Proposition 122; Detroit, Denver, Seattle and Washington D.C. have decriminalized possession. Decriminalization changes enforcement priority, not legality, and none of it covers buying, selling or mailing. Because microdosing means keeping a supply on hand over months, the exposure is a possession-quantity question rather than a per-dose one. Know your local laws.
