The µ-Opioid Receptor

Our research on the SR compounds — the structures, what we tested, and what nobody knows

This is where ÆTHER's own work on these compounds lives. Everything here is measured from publicly deposited structures and published papers; our analyses have not been peer reviewed, and the method is stated in each case so it can be checked. We sell SR-15099, so read it as an argument, not a report.

  • The structures — the receptor in 3D from real cryo-EM data, the binding pocket mapped residue by residue, and where SR-17018 actually sits in it.
  • What we tested and lost — the chlorine hypothesis, tight burial, a distinct receptor conformation. All measured, all refuted, kept here because the dead ends save someone else the walk.
  • The one clue that survived — SR-17018 touches the helix-7 face with a single atom where every other ligand uses 12–35% of its contacts, and helix 7 is the interface tied to tolerance.
  • The compound that is missing from all of this — SR-15099, the one we sell, has no deposited structure, no confirmed identity and no reference standard. Verified against a working control.
  • What is known, and the four things nobody has measured — including the unresolved contradiction over whether these compounds depress breathing.

For what a given compound does rather than how it works, the practical pages are SR-17018 and SR-15099. If you are using either to come off something, read SR vs Buprenorphine first.

Start With What Is Solved

Every opioid on this site acts on one protein: the µ-opioid receptor, a G-protein-coupled receptor threaded seven times through the membrane of a nerve cell. Its structure has been solved many times over, by X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, with different drugs held inside it.

The model below is real experimental data, not a drawing: the receptor from PDB entry 8EF5, a cryo-EM structure of the fentanyl-bound receptor coupled to its G protein, determined at 3.3 Å. The same study also solved the receptor with SR-17018 bound (8EFL), and with morphine (8EF6), so you can switch between the three or overlay them. The Pocket

Fentanyl sits in a cavity near the top of the receptor, the orthosteric site — the place the body's own endorphins bind. We measured which residues line it by taking every amino acid within 5 Å of the fentanyl molecule in 8EF5:

Gln126 · Asn129 · Trp135 · Val145 · Ile146 · Asp149 · Tyr150 · Met153 · Cys219 · Val238 · Trp295 · Ile298 · His299 · Val302 · Ile324 · Gly327 · Tyr328

Three of those do most of the work. Asp149 carries a negative charge that pairs with the positively charged nitrogen every classical opioid carries — morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone and SR-17018 all have one. Trp295 sits directly beneath the drug and shifts when the receptor switches on. Tyr328 caps the pocket from the other side.

This is the part worth understanding: a drug can be a powerful opioid and still differ enormously in what the receptor does after it binds. The pocket is the beginning of the story, not the end.

The Molecule

SR-17018 is 5,6-dichloro-3-[1-[(4-chlorophenyl)methyl]piperidin-4-yl]-1H-benzimidazol-2-one (C19H18Cl3N3O, 410.7 g/mol, PubChem CID 130431397). It looks nothing like morphine. It is a benzimidazolone joined to a piperidine ring, with a chlorobenzyl arm — a shape closer to the old benzimidazole opioids than to anything poppy-derived.

The 3D conformer below was generated from that structure and energy-minimized, with the piperidine nitrogen protonated as it would be at body pH. That nitrogen is the one that would reach for Asp149.

So Where Does It Bind?

In the same pocket as fentanyl. The same 2022 study that produced the fentanyl structure also froze the receptor with SR-17018 bound and solved it: PDB 8EFL, "SR17018-bound mu-opioid receptor-Gi complex". You can switch it on in the viewer above — that violet molecule is experimental data, not a guess.

We superimposed the two receptors on 287 backbone atoms (0.4 Å apart) so the two drugs can be compared in one frame. SR-17018's center of mass lands 1.4 Å from fentanyl's. Its contacts are the orthosteric residues listed above: Asp149 at 2.7 Å, then Val145, Trp135, Gln126, Tyr150, Asn129, Ile298, Met153, Cys219, Val238, Tyr328, His299, Val302 and Trp295. There is no separate site, no membrane-facing pocket, no second cavity. It sits where morphine sits.

That settles the location and sharpens the real question. In 2021 the Scripps group reported that SR-17018 is noncompetitive with radiolabelled antagonists and leaves the receptor in a G-protein-signalling state that survives washing, while remaining reversible by an antagonist in a living animal (Stahl et al., PNAS 2021). A drug that occupies the orthosteric pocket is not supposed to behave that way.

With the structure in hand, the explanation has to be about time rather than place. The compound appears to get into the ordinary pocket and then stay — stabilizing a receptor state it leaves slowly enough to look non-competing in an assay that assumes rapid equilibrium. A 2024 study from Dundee reached a similar conclusion from a different direction, under the plain title “Activation of µ receptors by SR-17018 through a distinctive mechanism”, and also found that much of the published "bias" in this field is an artifact of measuring in cells carrying far more receptors than a neuron does. The 2026 follow-up in Nature frames compounds of this class as selecting between active states that differ in how the receptor handles the G protein's nucleotide (Stahl et al., Nature 2026).

So: where it binds is answered. How long it stays, which state it holds, and what that does over months of use are not.

A Note on Models

Before we found 8EFL, this page carried a model of SR-17018 built by superimposing its piperidine ring onto fentanyl's and keeping the arrangement that fitted the pocket without collisions. You can still switch it on in the viewer, because the comparison is instructive.

It was close, and wrong in the way models usually are. The modeled molecule sits in the right pocket, with its charged nitrogen reaching toward Asp149 — but its center of mass is 2.6 Å from the experimental position, and its atoms average 2.3 Å from the nearest real one. At that scale the contacts are different, the ring orientations are different, and any conclusion drawn about how it binds would have been drawn from our own assumptions.

This is also why a docking score is a poor substitute for a structure. Docking would have placed the nitrogen near Asp149 too, because that is what scoring functions reward, and it would have produced a confident number for a pose that was still a couple of ångströms out. We only know the model was close because somebody did the experiment.

A Guess, and What Happened to It

Everything above this line is measured. What follows started as a guess, so we went and tested it, and it did not survive. We are leaving the whole thing here because the failure is more useful than the guess was.

The guess was about the chlorines. SR-17018 carries three of them, which is unusual, and in the structure each one is pressed against a different wall of the pocket. If those were halogen bonds — a real, directional interaction, not just two atoms touching — then leaving the pocket would mean breaking three of them at once plus the salt bridge to Asp149. A drug that binds normally but leaves slowly would look exactly the way SR-17018 looks in the laboratory: wash it off and the signal stays, run a competition assay built on fast exchange and it behaves as though it is not competing, give an antagonist to an animal and it still wins, because an animal has hours rather than minutes.

A halogen bond has a geometry you can check. The chlorine's positive patch sits directly opposite the carbon it is attached to, so the carbon, the chlorine and the oxygen it reaches for should fall nearly in a straight line, somewhere above about 155 degrees. We measured all four candidate contacts in 8EFL:

Every one of them approaches side-on, which is the opposite of what a halogen bond needs. They are ordinary contacts. The chlorines fill space and press against Val145, Trp135, Cys219 and His299, and that is all they do.

So we ran the broader version of the question. The same study solved the receptor with four other drugs bound, which gives a built-in comparison. If SR-17018 is held unusually tightly, it should show up as more buried surface or more contacts than the rest.

SR-17018 buries the least surface of the four large ligands and makes exactly as many contacts per atom as the others. On this evidence it is held no more tightly than fentanyl, and rather less than a drug nobody claims is wash-resistant.

The second idea fared no better. If SR-17018 holds the receptor in a distinctive shape, the difference ought to appear on the inside face, where the G protein docks. We fitted each structure to the fentanyl one using only the extracellular half, then measured how far the intracellular ends had drifted. SR-17018 came in at 0.50 Å, the same as morphine, while oliceridine managed 1.13 Å. Whatever is different about this compound, the receptor is not wearing it in a way that a 3.2 Å map can show.

At that point we had tested two guesses and lost both, so we went back to the paper that produced the structures and read what its authors had actually concluded. They were not looking at how hard the pocket grips. They were looking at which side of it each drug leans on.

The pocket has two faces. One is built from helix 3, the other from helices 6 and 7. Morphine and fentanyl touch both. We counted every receptor atom within 4.5 Å of the drug in eight published structures, taking the helix-7 face to mean the three residues at its top — in human numbering Ile324, Gly327 and Tyr328. Because the studies number their residues differently, each structure was anchored on two conserved landmarks found by geometry rather than by number: the aspartate that makes the salt bridge, and the tyrosine at the top of helix 7.

SR-17018 is on its own. The next lowest is morphine at 13%, six times more, and SR-17018 never reaches Ile324 or Gly327 at all. Press the Highlight helix 7 button in the viewer and switch between the drugs; the gap is easier to see than to read about.

The authors of the Cell paper made the same observation about their own structures and went further: mutate that helix 6/7 interface and morphine and fentanyl stop recruiting arrestin, the protein blamed for much of what opioids do badly. Drugs that avoid touching it favor G-protein signalling instead.

The last row is there for a particular reason. Buprenorphine is the approved medicine for opioid use disorder, and it shares SR-17018's most distinctive measured property: both leave the receptor chemically marked for hours after washout, where ordinary agonists clear in minutes. There is no structure of buprenorphine bound to this receptor — none has ever been deposited, which is startling for a drug prescribed since 2002. BU72 is the closest published relative, built on the same thebaine-derived core and differing at the side chain and the nitrogen. It sits with morphine at 12%, six times more helix-7 contact than SR-17018.

That is worth holding onto, carefully. The two drugs that behave alike in the washout experiment part company here: on this one axis the buprenorphine scaffold is unremarkable and SR-17018 is extreme. And helix 7 is the face whose mutation abolishes arrestin recruitment — the pathway tied to tolerance and desensitization. In mice, SR-17018 reverses morphine tolerance where buprenorphine suppresses withdrawal but maintains tolerance (Grim et al., 2020). The compound that undoes tolerance is the compound that does not touch the tolerance interface.

We like that too much to trust it. BU72 is a relative, not buprenorphine. One structural axis does not carry a mechanism. And it is the kind of neat story that the chlorine hypothesis further up this page also told, right before it fell apart. It is a hypothesis worth testing, and it is not a finding. The comparison with buprenorphine sets out what is actually established about the two.

The tidy version of that story does not survive the wider comparison. Oliceridine is the one drug in this table that reached the clinic on its G-protein bias, and it presses on helix 7 harder than fentanyl does. Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, the kratom-derived compound usually described the same way, touches all three residues too. Morphine, which nobody calls biased, sits near the bottom. Whatever helix 7 contributes, it does not sort these drugs into the categories the labels promise — and SR-17018 is still the outlier by a distance.

One more thing follows from all this, and we measured it rather than assumed it. If SR-17018 ignores a whole face of the pocket, either the receptor closes around it or a gap is left. We laid a grid through each structure and counted the buried empty space with the drug in place. SR-17018 sits in the roomiest pocket of the set, 838 Å³ against fentanyl's 731, and leaves 412 Å³ of buried space unfilled — as much volume again as the drug itself occupies, and half as much again as oliceridine leaves. The receptor does not close around this molecule. Something else is in there: ordered water, an ion, or nothing at all. A structure at 3.2 Å cannot say which, and that gap is a reasonable place for the next experiment to look.

A 2021 study adds the one measurement that bears on timing. SR-17018 provokes a pattern of receptor phosphorylation indistinguishable from a full, unbiased agonist, except that where the normal pattern reverses within minutes of washing the drug away, SR-17018's persisted for hours (Fritzwanker et al., 2021). That is the closest anyone has come to the thing we guessed at from the chlorines, and it was found by watching the clock rather than the coordinates.

So the honest summary: our chlorine story was wrong, the pocket grips this drug no harder than it grips fentanyl, and the real difference is a face of the pocket that SR-17018 simply does not touch. Whether that is why it behaves strangely in a living animal is still an open question, and it is being argued about in the literature right now.

One caveat on all of the above. Both structures come from cryo-electron microscopy at around 3.2 Å, good enough to place a molecule and not good enough to defend a bond length to a tenth of an ångström. A real difference smaller than about half an ångström would be invisible here, so "we cannot see it" is not the same as "it is not there".

The Compound That Is Missing From All of This

Everything above is about SR-17018. The compound ÆTHER actually sells is SR-15099, and it does not appear anywhere in this page's evidence — because there is nothing to show. We checked the Protein Data Bank directly, with a working control to make sure the search itself was sound: a query for fentanyl returns 42 structures, a query for SR17018 returns exactly one, 8EFL. A query for SR15099 returns nothing at all. No structure of it bound to any receptor has ever been deposited.

So every structural statement about SR-15099 — including the ones on our own SR-15099 page — is an inference from its close relative, not an observation of it. That inference is reasonable and it is not evidence.

It gets thinner. The identity of the material is not firmly established either. A CAS registry number exists and is well formed — 2134602-44-9, sitting immediately before SR-17018's 2134602-45-0, which is consistent with the two having been registered together. It does not resolve in PubChem or ChEMBL. Tying structure to registry number to name needs paid CAS registry access, which we have not bought. And no certified reference standard is published, which has a blunt practical consequence: a testing laboratory has nothing to calibrate against, so no lab can currently verify a sample of it — ours or anyone else's.

We could put a model of it in the viewer. We are not going to, for the reason set out in A Note on Models further up: when we modelled SR-17018 before the real structure appeared, the model was 2.6 Å out at the centre of mass, which was close enough to look convincing and far enough to make every conclusion about contacts wrong. Doing that again for a compound whose structure is itself uncertain would stack one guess on another and render it in 3D, which is the most persuasive possible way to be wrong.

Two things would close this gap. Cryo-EM of SR-15099 at the receptor, which is beyond what we can do. Or analytical characterisation of market samples — identity, purity, analogue content, batch variation — which needs money rather than permission, involves no human subjects and therefore no ethics board, and is the single most publishable piece of work available in this area. Nobody has done it.

What Is Actually Known About the Effects

Structural elegance is not safety. The animal and human record, briefly:

  • It is reinforcing. In rats trained to self-administer drugs, SR-17018 and SR-14968 functioned as reinforcers — less so than oxycodone and fentanyl, but they were taken (Zamarripa et al., 2024). "G-protein biased" does not mean "non-addictive".
  • Respiratory depression: two laboratories, opposite answers. In the same study, oxycodone and fentanyl produced dose-dependent respiratory depression and the SR compounds did not. That is the central claim for this class. It is also contradicted. Measuring breathing in awake mice by plethysmography, Hill et al., 2023 found oral SR-17018 depressed respiration dose-dependently, comparably to morphine, oxycodone and methadone at the top of the range — and that when doses were matched for equal respiratory depression, every opioid tested gave equivalent analgesia. On that reading the safety margin the class is named for did not appear at all. The contradiction has not been resolved, and settling it needs a head-to-head in one laboratory with the same species, route and dose range. Of everything unknown about this compound, this is the one that matters most.
  • Tolerance and withdrawal in mice. Repeated SR-17018 did not produce the tolerance morphine does, and it reversed established morphine tolerance while preventing withdrawal signs (Grim et al., 2020). This is the finding behind most of what is said about these compounds online. It is mice.
  • No clinical trial has ever been run. SR-17018 never left the laboratory. There is no approved human dose, no pharmacokinetic data in people, and no established treatment for an overdose beyond general opioid management.
  • It is being sold and used. A 2026 review in Cureus describes internet sales of SR-17018 as "research grade compound", a DEA alert about such compounds, and an emergency physician's account of a suspected overdose (Raffa et al., 2026).
  • The only human data is self-reported. A registry of 55 people who used SR-17018, most with several failed detox attempts, reported high rates of improvement — and its own author concludes the design "precludes any conclusion regarding efficacy or safety" and that the sample is biased toward good outcomes (Picard, 2026).

Naloxone reverses these compounds in animals, and the Scripps group specifically noted that antagonist reversal is retained. If you are around anyone using opioids of any kind, keep naloxone in the room. See the crisis guide.

Four things nobody has measured, which belong next to all of the above:

  • Whether naloxone restores breathing. It reverses the receptor-level effects in a dish. Nobody has run plethysmography with naloxone rescue, so the question an emergency physician would actually ask has no answer.
  • Any human dose. Liver-microsome half-life runs around 3.9 hours in human preparations against 0.6 hours in mouse. Animal doses do not transfer, and the sixfold gap runs in the direction of accumulation. Every dose in community use is guesswork, ours included.
  • What happens on stopping. Nothing published. It is the question people ask most.
  • Whether a sample can be verified. No certified reference standard for SR-15099 is published, so a testing laboratory has nothing to calibrate against.

We sell SR-15099, so take none of this on trust — the sources are linked precisely so the argument can be checked against us. And one limit worth stating outright: the store's safety-feedback form is there to warn people, not to collect data. Research on people needs an ethics board, consent, and someone who owns the data and has no stake in the product, and selling the compound disqualifies us from the last of those permanently.

Key Papers

The structural and receptor-level work. For the compound-level evidence — the animal studies, the overdose report and the human registry — see the SR-17018 page, which lists them in full.

  1. Medicinal chemistry Bias factor and therapeutic window correlate to predict safer opioid analgesics

    Schmid et al. · 2017 · Cell 171:1165 · PubMed

    The paper that introduced SR-17018 and its relatives, from Scripps Research. It proposed that the separation between pain relief and respiratory depression tracks with signalling bias — the idea the whole class rests on, and one later work has qualified.

  2. Receptor pharmacology G protein signaling-biased mu opioid receptor agonists that produce sustained G protein activation are noncompetitive agonists

    Stahl et al. · 2021 · PNAS 118:e2102178118 · PubMed

    The key paper for the binding-site question. SR-17018 and SR-14968 do not compete with radiolabelled antagonists and leave the receptor in a wash-resistant signalling state, while remaining reversible by antagonists in vivo.

  3. Receptor pharmacology Activation of µ receptors by SR-17018 through a distinctive mechanism

    Singleton et al. · 2024 · Neuropharmacology 258:110093 · PubMed

    Re-measured a dozen opioids with receptor numbers cut down to realistic levels, and found that much published "bias" reflects receptor reserve in overexpressing cells. SR-17018 still behaved differently from the rest.

  4. Receptor pharmacology GTP release-selective agonists prolong opioid analgesic efficacy

    Stahl et al. · 2026 · Nature 650:500 · PubMed

    The current account of what makes these compounds unusual, now that the binding site is settled: agonists can prefer receptor states that differ in how the G protein releases rather than binds its nucleotide. In mice, a release-preferring compound extended morphine and fentanyl pain relief without adding to fentanyl's respiratory effects.

  5. Animal study Comparison of the reinforcing, antinociceptive, and respiratory depressant effects of prototypical and G-protein biased µ-opioid receptor agonists

    Zamarripa et al. · 2024 · Psychopharmacology 241:2453 · PubMed

    Rats self-administered SR-17018 and SR-14968, so the compounds have abuse liability, though less than oxycodone or fentanyl. Neither produced respiratory depression at the doses tested, while the conventional opioids did.

  6. Structure Molecular recognition of morphine and fentanyl by the human µ-opioid receptor

    Zhuang et al. · 2022 · Cell 185:4361 · PDB 8EF5

    The source of both structures on this page. The group froze the human receptor with five different drugs bound and solved each one, including fentanyl (8EF5) and SR-17018 (8EFL). It is the experiment that answers where SR-17018 binds: the ordinary orthosteric pocket, not a separate site.
