The µ-Opioid Receptor

What the structures show, and what they do not

Every opioid on this site acts on one protein. It has been solved many times by cryo-electron microscopy, with different drugs held inside it, and those structures are public. This page shows them, and sets out what we have measured from them.

The analysis is ours and it is not peer reviewed. The working detail sits on the notes page: the hypotheses that failed, the methods, the limits and the references.

What We Found

  • It binds the ordinary pocket. SR-17018 sits where morphine sits, 1.4 Å from fentanyl’s centre of mass, with Asp149 at 2.7 Å. There is no second site and no membrane-facing cavity.
  • It ignores one face of that pocket. SR-17018 touches the helix-7 triad with a single atom, 2% of its contacts. Every other ligand measured uses 12 to 35%. Helix 7 is the interface whose mutation abolishes arrestin recruitment, and arrestin signalling is tied to tolerance.
  • Four explanations for its odd behaviour were tested, and all four failed. Halogen bonding, tighter binding, a distinct receptor shape, a second site. None survives measurement, which suggests the mechanism is not visible in a static structure at all.
  • SR-15099 has no structure. Nothing bound to any receptor has ever been deposited for it, and no reference standard is published, so no laboratory can verify a sample.
  • Two laboratories disagree about breathing. One reports no respiratory depression at tested doses. Hill et al., 2023 measured awake mice and found it dose-dependent, comparable to morphine at the top of the range. The dispute is current rather than settled: a December 2025 Nature paper from the originating laboratory restates that SR-17018 “produces antinociception without respiratory suppression”. Both positions are in print at once, which is the most important open question here.

A mechanism has since been proposed, and it does not solve this. Stahl et al. (Nature, 2025) describe opioid receptors as running the nucleotide exchange in both directions, and sort agonists by whether they prefer to drive GTP binding or GTP release. SR-17018 fits: the authors note it is “also release-preferring”. But the paper states plainly that “competitive interactions are preserved” between orthosteric agonists and antagonists, and its companion adds that “the release reaction is competitive with antagonists”. The non-competitive behaviour is mentioned only as an earlier finding and is never connected to the new mechanism. The anomaly on this page is still unexplained.

The Helix-7 Count

SR-17018 is on its own. The next lowest is morphine at 13%, six times more. Press Highlight helix 7 in the viewer above and switch between the drugs.

This is the one structural clue that survived testing, and it is a hypothesis rather than a finding. The table also refuses to behave: oliceridine, the biased agonist that reached the clinic, presses helix 7 harder than fentanyl does, and morphine sits near the bottom. Whatever helix 7 contributes, it does not sort these drugs into the categories the labels promise.

The Two Compounds

SR-15099 is SR-17018 with one atom changed: bromine instead of chlorine at the end of the benzyl arm. Switch between them and that atom is the only thing that moves. A great deal is claimed about the difference.

What We Are Doing Now

The structural work has run out of road. A still photograph of the bound state does not contain the answer, so the next useful experiments measure time rather than shape, and we cannot run those.

Two things we can do. The first is analytical: sending market samples for identity, purity, analogue content and batch variation. Nobody has done this, it needs money rather than permission, and it would answer a question this page says is open — whether what circulates under these names is what the label claims.

The second is asking for real data from suppliers rather than a certificate that asserts a result without showing it. Spectra, chromatograms, methods, batch numbers on the printout.

Four questions stay open and none of them needs new chemistry: whether naloxone restores breathing, what a human dose is, what happens when people stop, and whether a sample can be verified at all. Each is measurable on the compounds that already exist.
