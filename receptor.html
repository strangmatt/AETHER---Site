The µ-Opioid Receptor

The structures, the hypotheses tested and refuted, and what nobody knows

The Receptor

Every opioid on this site acts on one protein: the µ-opioid receptor, threaded seven times through the membrane of a nerve cell. It has been solved many times over, with different drugs held inside it.

Seventeen residues line the pocket. Three do most of the work: Asp149 carries the negative charge that pairs with the positive nitrogen every classical opioid has; Trp295 sits beneath the drug and shifts when the receptor switches on; Tyr328 caps the pocket from the far side.

The point worth holding onto: a drug can be a powerful opioid and still differ enormously in what the receptor does after it binds. The pocket is the beginning of the story, not the end.

Two Molecules, One Atom Apart

SR-17018 is 5,6-dichloro-3-[1-[(4-chlorophenyl)methyl]piperidin-4-yl]-1H-benzimidazol-2-one (C19H18Cl3N3O, 410.7 g/mol, PubChem CID 130431397). It looks nothing like morphine — a benzimidazolone joined to a piperidine ring with a halobenzyl arm, closer to the old benzimidazole opioids than to anything poppy-derived.

SR-15099 is the same molecule with one atom changed — bromine for chlorine at the end of the benzyl arm (C19H18BrCl2N3O, 455.2 g/mol, CID 130431412). Switch between them below; the only thing that moves is that one atom. A great deal is claimed about the difference.

Where It Binds

In the same pocket as fentanyl. The violet molecule in the viewer is experimental data: SR-17018’s centre of mass lands 1.4 Å from fentanyl’s, with Asp149 at 2.7 Å. No separate site, no second cavity. It sits where morphine sits.

That settles the location and sharpens the real question. SR-17018 is noncompetitive with radiolabelled antagonists and leaves the receptor in a signalling state that survives washing, while still being reversible by an antagonist in a living animal (Stahl et al., PNAS 2021). A drug occupying the ordinary pocket is not supposed to behave that way.

With the structure in hand, the explanation has to be about time rather than place — getting into the ordinary pocket and then staying, slowly enough to look non-competing in an assay that assumes rapid equilibrium. Where it binds is answered. How long it stays, and what that does over months of use, are not.

Four Explanations, All Refuted

If the drug lingers, something should hold it there. Four explanations were tested against the deposited coordinates. All four failed, and they are kept here because a dead end nobody publishes is a dead end everyone walks down twice.

1. The chlorines form halogen bonds. SR-17018 carries three, each pressed against a different wall. A halogen bond is directional: the carbon, the halogen and its partner should fall nearly in a straight line, within about 25°. All four candidate contacts in 8EFL:

Every one approaches side-on. They are ordinary contacts that fill space, not bonds. The same holds for SR-15099: bromine is the better halogen-bond donor, so if the idea worked anywhere in this series it should work there — modelled, its contact sits 47° off straight, no better aligned than the chlorine it replaces.

2. It is held more tightly than the others. If so, it should bury more surface or make more contacts. It does neither:

SR-17018 buries the least surface of the four large ligands and makes as many contacts per atom as the rest. It is held no more tightly than fentanyl.

3. It holds the receptor in a distinct shape. Fitting each structure on the extracellular half and measuring how far the intracellular ends drift, SR-17018 lands 0.50 Å from fentanyl’s — the same as morphine, while oliceridine manages 1.13 Å. 4. It occupies a second site. Empty adjacent volume is large in every structure (1,200–2,000 Å³, the extracellular vestibule) and no different here.

The conclusion is itself the finding: the mechanism is probably not visible in a static structure at all. A still photograph of the bound state does not contain it. That points at kinetics, or at a receptor state outliving the drug — and it means the next useful experiments are time-resolved rather than structural. The one measurement that bears on timing: receptor phosphorylation persists for hours after washout where a full agonist’s clears in minutes, and the only other compound tested that behaved that way was buprenorphine (Fritzwanker et al., 2021).

Method. Coordinates from the RCSB PDB; contacts as unique receptor atoms within 4 Å per ligand atom; buried surface by Shrake–Rupley with a 1.4 Å probe. Two caveats on all of it. These are cryo-EM structures near 3.2 Å — good enough to place a molecule, not to defend a bond length, so a real difference under about half an ångström would be invisible. And before 8EFL existed this page carried a model of SR-17018 built onto fentanyl’s pose; it was 2.6 Å out at the centre of mass, close enough to look convincing and far enough that every conclusion about contacts was wrong. You can still switch it on in the viewer.

The One Clue That Survived

The pocket has two faces — one built from helix 3, the other from helices 6 and 7. Morphine and fentanyl touch both. Every receptor atom within 4.5 Å of the drug was counted across eight published structures, each anchored on conserved landmarks found by geometry rather than by residue number.

SR-17018 is on its own. The next lowest is morphine at 13%, six times more, and SR-17018 never reaches Ile324 or Gly327 at all. Press Highlight helix 7 in the viewer and switch between the drugs.

Why it might matter: helix 7 is the interface whose mutation abolishes arrestin recruitment, and arrestin signalling is tied to desensitisation and tolerance. In mice SR-17018 reverses established morphine tolerance where buprenorphine suppresses withdrawal but maintains it (Grim et al., 2020). The compound that undoes tolerance is the compound that does not touch the tolerance interface.

It is a tidy story, which is reason enough to distrust it. It is the kind of neat story the chlorine hypothesis also told, right before it fell apart. And the wider table refuses to cooperate: oliceridine, the one drug here that reached the clinic on its G-protein bias, presses helix 7 harder than fentanyl does, while morphine sits near the bottom. Whatever helix 7 contributes, it does not sort these drugs into the categories the labels promise. It is a hypothesis worth testing, and it is not a finding.

SR-15099: No Structure, No Standard

Everything above is SR-17018. The compound ÆTHER sells appears nowhere in that evidence, because there is nothing to show. The Protein Data Bank was queried with a control to confirm the search itself worked: fentanyl returns 42 structures, SR17018 returns exactly one, SR15099 returns nothing. No structure of it bound to any receptor has ever been deposited.

Its identity, however, is better established than this page previously stated. The name does not resolve in PubChem or ChEMBL — but the structure does, as PubChem CID 130431412 and CHEMBL4217435, carrying µ-opioid data (EC50 149 nM, Emax 83%) that match the published SR-15099 figures of 155 nM and 81%. The CAS number 2134602-44-9 sits immediately before SR-17018’s 2134602-45-0, as two compounds registered together would.

What that does not fix: no certified reference standard is published, so a testing laboratory has nothing to calibrate against and no lab can currently verify a sample — ours or anyone’s. Knowing what the molecule is does not tell you what is in the bag.

Because the two differ by one atom, the measured SR-17018 pose can be swapped at that position and the change measured. This is not a prediction of how SR-15099 binds; it is the narrower question of what that substitution does to the geometry with everything else held at its experimental position.

That needs money rather than permission, involves no human subjects, and nobody has done it.

What Is Known About the Effects

Structural elegance is not safety. The record, briefly:

  • It is reinforcing. Rats self-administered SR-17018 and SR-14968 — less than oxycodone and fentanyl, but they took them (Zamarripa et al., 2024). “G-protein biased” does not mean “non-addictive”.
  • Breathing: two laboratories, opposite answers. One group reports no respiratory depression at tested doses, which is the central claim for this class. Measuring awake mice by plethysmography, Hill et al., 2023 found oral SR-17018 depressed breathing dose-dependently, comparably to morphine and methadone at the top of the range — and at doses matched for equal respiratory depression, every opioid tested gave equivalent analgesia. On that reading the safety margin the class is named for did not appear. Unresolved, and the thing that matters most.
  • Tolerance in mice. Repeated SR-17018 produced no tolerance and reversed morphine’s while preventing withdrawal (Grim et al., 2020). This is the finding behind most of what is said online. It is mice, from the laboratory holding the patent.
  • No clinical trial has ever been run. No approved human dose, no pharmacokinetics in people, no established overdose treatment beyond general opioid management.
  • The only human data is self-reported. A registry of 55 users reported high rates of improvement — and its author concludes the design “precludes any conclusion regarding efficacy or safety” (Picard, 2026).

Naloxone reverses these compounds in animals. If you are around anyone using opioids of any kind, keep it in the room — see the crisis guide. Four things nobody has measured:

  • Whether naloxone restores breathing. It reverses receptor-level effects in a dish. Nobody has run plethysmography with naloxone rescue, so the question an emergency physician would ask has no answer.
  • Any human dose. Liver-microsome half-life is ~3.9 hours in human preparations against 0.6 in mouse. Animal doses do not transfer, and the gap runs toward accumulation. Every dose in community use is guesswork, ours included.
  • What happens on stopping. Nothing published. The question people ask most.
  • Whether a sample can be verified. No reference standard, so no.

One limit stated outright: the store’s safety-feedback form exists to warn people, not to collect data. Research on people needs an ethics board, consent, and someone who owns the data with no stake in the product. Selling the compound disqualifies ÆTHER from the last of those permanently.

Key Papers

The structural and receptor-level work. For the compound-level evidence — the animal studies, the overdose report and the human registry — see the SR-17018 page, which lists them in full.

  1. Medicinal chemistry Bias factor and therapeutic window correlate to predict safer opioid analgesics

    Schmid et al. · 2017 · Cell 171:1165 · PubMed

    The paper that introduced SR-17018 and its relatives, from Scripps Research. It proposed that the separation between pain relief and respiratory depression tracks with signalling bias — the idea the whole class rests on, and one later work has qualified.

  2. Receptor pharmacology G protein signaling-biased mu opioid receptor agonists that produce sustained G protein activation are noncompetitive agonists

    Stahl et al. · 2021 · PNAS 118:e2102178118 · PubMed

    The key paper for the binding-site question. SR-17018 and SR-14968 do not compete with radiolabelled antagonists and leave the receptor in a wash-resistant signalling state, while remaining reversible by antagonists in vivo.

  3. Receptor pharmacology Activation of µ receptors by SR-17018 through a distinctive mechanism

    Singleton et al. · 2024 · Neuropharmacology 258:110093 · PubMed

    Re-measured a dozen opioids with receptor numbers cut down to realistic levels, and found that much published "bias" reflects receptor reserve in overexpressing cells. SR-17018 still behaved differently from the rest.

  4. Receptor pharmacology GTP release-selective agonists prolong opioid analgesic efficacy

    Stahl et al. · 2026 · Nature 650:500 · PubMed

    The current account of what makes these compounds unusual, now that the binding site is settled: agonists can prefer receptor states that differ in how the G protein releases rather than binds its nucleotide. In mice, a release-preferring compound extended morphine and fentanyl pain relief without adding to fentanyl's respiratory effects.

  5. Animal study Comparison of the reinforcing, antinociceptive, and respiratory depressant effects of prototypical and G-protein biased µ-opioid receptor agonists

    Zamarripa et al. · 2024 · Psychopharmacology 241:2453 · PubMed

    Rats self-administered SR-17018 and SR-14968, so the compounds have abuse liability, though less than oxycodone or fentanyl. Neither produced respiratory depression at the doses tested, while the conventional opioids did.

  6. Structure Molecular recognition of morphine and fentanyl by the human µ-opioid receptor

    Zhuang et al. · 2022 · Cell 185:4361 · PDB 8EF5

    The source of both structures on this page. The group froze the human receptor with five different drugs bound and solved each one, including fentanyl (8EF5) and SR-17018 (8EFL). It is the experiment that answers where SR-17018 binds: the ordinary orthosteric pocket, not a separate site.
